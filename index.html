Introduction¶

Our names are Benjamin Hogg and Elijah Stolte, and we are studying datasets related to food recalls in the United States. The datasets present include information on food recall reporting and incidents that are related to food recalls. One is a dataset of current food recalls in the US and the second is an FDA dataset of recalls in the US beginning in 2004 (though the data really only includes relevant information from 2013 to now). We also have a dataset including information of adverse food events from 2013 to now. In this tutorial we will explore the trends among the types of recalls most prevelent now and compare them to trends over time. We will also look at food event reporting and see what kinds of problems were most common and resulted in the most recalls.

Here are the datasets we will be working with:

  1. https://dailyfoodrecalls.com/api/recalls.json
    • This is a dataset that lists every current food recall active in the United States. This is interesting due to the current relevance that food recalls have on our public, most notably with the beef recall happening due to lax foreign import rules.
  2. https://open.fda.gov/apis/food/enforcement/
    • This dataset from the FDA reports data from their Recall Enterprise System which contains information on recall events from 2004 to present. This data will provide us with historical context of FDA food recalls so that we can compare trends in kinds of recalls over time or seasonally. We can also use this data to observe the relationship between these recalls with adverse event reports.
  3. https://open.fda.gov/apis/food/event/
    • This dataset from the FDA reports data from the Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition Adverse Event Reporting System (CAERS). It contains information on adverse events and product complaint reports submitted for foods, dietary supplements, and cosmetics. The food data within this dataset is useful for our questioning as we can explore how food recalls are created and wether or not these adverse events precede or follow the initiation of the recalls.

Using these datasets, we plan to analyze the trends in food recalls such as how problems lead to food recalls being initiated, what kinds of trends are present with the problems that are the most common or problematic, and if the food recalls have an effect on adverse-event reporting. We can also observe changes in these things over time and look at how they vary by geographical location.

As of November 14, 2026, the raw data has 302 recalls and 35 columns (this is a live feed, so the count changes over time). Many of these are metadata (URL's, internal scores) that don't do anything for us.

We decided to only keep the columns for:

  • product & company (who made it and what is it)
  • which agency (FDA or USDA)
  • class (how bad the health consequences are)
  • classInferred (if the website guessed or got it straight from the agency)
  • hazard (what the actual problem is)
  • allergens (allergens)
  • affectedStates (which states the recall affects)
  • recall type (voluntary or agency requested)
  • recall initiated & report date (when the recall happened and when it was reported)

Data Analysis 3¶

The CAERS data is not only food. It also covers dietary supplements and cosmetics, so before analyzing anything we label each report by the type of product it is about. Our project is about food recalls, so most of this section focuses on reports about conventional foods.

The analysis below has three goals:

  1. Understand who files food adverse event reports and how serious they are.
  2. See what reactions people report about food.
  3. Check how adverse event reports line up with FDA recalls over time and across the year, since this is the basis for both of our model questions.

Preparing the data for analysis¶

A few more steps are needed before we can analyze the reports:

  • Product group: each product has an FDA industry_name. We group them into Cosmetics, Supplements (Vit/Min/Prot/Unconv Diet) and Food (everything else). Each report is labeled by its first SUSPECT product, the product the reporter believes caused the problem (as opposed to a CONCOMITANT product that was only being used at the same time).
  • Age: ages are given in mixed units (years, months, weeks, days, decades), so we convert everything to years.
  • Outcomes and reactions: a single report can list several outcomes and reactions, so we make tidy tables with one row per (report, outcome) and per (report, reaction). The reaction names mix capitalization (VOMITING vs Vomiting), so we standardize them to uppercase.
  • Serious outcome flag: a report is marked serious if any of its outcomes is a death, life-threatening event, hospitalization, disability or emergency room visit.

# Label each product as Cosmetics, Supplements, or Food based on its FDA industry name
def product_group(industry):
    if industry == "Cosmetics":
        return "Cosmetics"
    if industry == "Vit/Min/Prot/Unconv Diet(Human/Animal)":
        return "Supplements"
    return "Food"

event_products["product_group"] = event_products["industry_name"].apply(product_group)

# Each report gets the group of its first SUSPECT product (the product believed to cause the event)
report_group = (event_products[event_products["role"] == "SUSPECT"]
                .drop_duplicates("report_number")
                .set_index("report_number")["product_group"])
events["product_group"] = events["report_number"].map(report_group).astype("category")

# Age comes in mixed units, so convert everything to years
unit_to_years = {"year(s)": 1, "decade(s)": 10, "month(s)": 1/12, "week(s)": 1/52, "day(s)": 1/365}
events["age_years"] = pd.to_numeric(events["age"], errors="coerce") * events["age_units"].map(unit_to_years)
events["gender"] = events["gender"].astype("category")

# Tidy tables: one row per (report, outcome) and one row per (report, reaction)
event_outcomes = events[["report_number", "outcomes"]].explode("outcomes").dropna()
event_reactions = events[["report_number", "reactions"]].explode("reactions").dropna()
event_reactions["reactions"] = event_reactions["reactions"].str.upper()  # "Vomiting" and "VOMITING" are the same reaction

# Flag reports that had a serious outcome, or a death
serious_outcomes = ["Death", "Life Threatening", "Hospitalization", "Disability", "Visited Emergency Room"]
serious_reports = set(event_outcomes.loc[event_outcomes["outcomes"].isin(serious_outcomes), "report_number"])
death_reports = set(event_outcomes.loc[event_outcomes["outcomes"] == "Death", "report_number"])
events["serious"] = events["report_number"].isin(serious_reports)
events["death"] = events["report_number"].isin(death_reports)

# Most of our analysis only looks at conventional food
food_events = events[events["product_group"] == "Food"].copy()
food_reactions = event_reactions[event_reactions["report_number"].isin(food_events["report_number"])]

print(events["product_group"].value_counts())

Graph 2: Do adverse event reports line up with recalls over time?¶

This is where we bring Data Sources 2 and 3 together. For every month from 2013 to 2025 we count the food adverse event reports (CAERS) and the FDA food recall events (enforcement data). We count unique recall event_ids instead of rows, because one recall event can cover dozens of individual products and would otherwise look like a big spike.

The two series are drawn as separate panels on the same timeline because they are measured on different scales.

What the graph shows:

  • Month to month, the two series barely move together (correlation of about 0.1). Most months, adverse event reports sit around 150 no matter how many recalls there are.
  • The biggest adverse event spikes are caused by single high-profile products. The table below the graph lists the top brands in the spike months: Jif peanut butter in May 2022 (a Salmonella recall), Lucky Charms cereal in April 2022 (a wave of GI complaints) and Quorn meat substitutes in January 2017.

So the relationship between recalls and adverse events probably shows up at the level of individual products or brands, not in total monthly counts. This shapes how we will set up Model Question 1.

Graph 3: Are food adverse event reports seasonal?¶

Model Question 2 asks whether problems can be predicted by time of year, so we check whether food reports follow a seasonal pattern. Each box shows the spread of monthly report counts for that calendar month across 2013 to 2025. The line in the middle of each box is the median, and the dots are unusual months.

The medians are fairly flat across the year, around 140 to 180 reports a month. The highest dots are the single-product events from Graph 2: Lucky Charms in April 2022, Quorn in January 2017 and Jif in May 2022. The Jif month (1,746 reports) is so far above the rest that we cut the y-axis at 800 so the boxes stay readable. If we had only looked at the mean for each month, those two events would have made spring look like a high-risk season, which is why a boxplot (medians and spread) is the better choice here.

Overall, the volume of food reports does not appear to be strongly seasonal. A seasonal pattern might still show up for specific causes, like Salmonella in recalls, so Model Question 2 should focus on a specific hazard instead of all reports combined.

What this tells us for our model questions¶

  • Model 1 (recalls vs. adverse events): total monthly counts are only weakly related (r ≈ 0.1), but the biggest spikes in reports come from specific products that were recalled or investigated. A better model would match adverse events to recalls by brand or product and look at report counts in the weeks before and after a recall starts.
  • Model 2 (time of year): overall food report volume is not strongly seasonal once the outlier events are accounted for. We should test seasonality for a specific hazard (for example Salmonella or Listeria recalls, or GI reactions) instead of for all reports together.
  • Because GI symptoms make up most food reports (Graph 1), they are the best adverse event signal to connect to pathogen recalls.

Plausible Model Questions¶

  1. Is there a predictable relationship between reported recalls and adverse events?
  • First we will see whether increases in adverse event reports precede, coincide with, or follow reported recalls. Based on what we find, we will see if we can correctly predict when a food recall will be filed or when adverse events might begin appearing.
  1. Can recalls and adverse events for a given cause be predicted relative to the time of year?
  • We will plot monthly recall counts for specific hazards like Salmonella and Listeria to look for seasonal peaks, since Graph 3 shows total adverse event reports aren't strongly seasonal on their own. For hazards that do show a pattern, we will train a model on past years and test it on later years to predict how many recalls to expect at a given time of year.
